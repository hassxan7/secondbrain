How it works

From the outside.

  1. Someone starts a plan — adds people by number or email, optionally seeds a few ideas.
  2. Everyone gets a link — name, suburb, budget, what they're up for, when they're free.
  3. Anyone can add to it — suggest something, or paste an event link.
  4. The plan computes itself — time, activities, cost, and where to meet.
  5. It lands in a calendar — and that's where installing Ripple starts paying off.

Under the hood

The parts that were harder than they look.

1 · Scheduling

Every candidate slot is scored as a weighted sum over participants. Three things make it more than a headcount:

Weight falls with absenteeism. Someone who never shows up moves the schedule less, floored so they're never erased. The instinct is the opposite — weight the no-shows more so the time suits them — but that rewards the behaviour and lets one person drag a seven-person plan around indefinitely.

Silence scores zero, never a maybe. An unanswered slot cannot inflate a score, so the fastest way to change the answer is to go and get an answer.

A recurring slot has hysteresis. An incumbent time carries a stability bonus and has to be beaten by a margin before anything moves. A weekly thing that relocates every week stops being a schelling point and attendance collapses.

Timezone maths is Intl-only, so the same code runs in the Worker, in Node and in the browser. Sydney DST goes through the IANA database rather than a fixed offset — a fortnight grid straddling the October change keeps 7:30pm at 7:30pm while the underlying UTC instant shifts.

2 · The ballot model

This is the interesting one. The plan fills in asynchronously, so the third person to answer can add an option the first two never saw. Three obvious handlings, all wrong:

  • Re-open the poll — everyone redoes everything; completion collapses and the prompt people are punished.
  • Ignore the gap — the new option only collects votes from whoever answers after it, so late options can never win and suggesting is pointless.
  • Assume approval — manufactures consent for something nobody agreed to.

The fix is to stop treating an answer as one submission and treat it as one cell per option, each either answered or pending. Adding an option doesn't invalidate anyone's existing answers — it opens exactly one new pending cell per person.

Here's the state right after Ross suggests something:

OptionIsaacKezRossStatus
Pool ✓ ✓ ✓ Eligible · 3 votes
Korean BBQ · ✓ · Eligible · 1 vote
Yochi · · ✓ Eligible · 1 vote
Night market ? ? ✓ Waiting on 2
✓ wants it · saw it, passed ? never shown

Two properties fall out of this, and both matter.

Re-asking is proportional. Isaac and Kez are shown exactly one question — Night market — not the whole form again. A few seconds, not a re-run. The API exposes this directly:

GET /api/ripple/hangouts/:id/asks?token=…
→ { "count": 1, "asks": [{ "label": "Night market",
                           "origin": { "kind": "suggested", "by": "ross" } }] }

An option can't win before people have seen it. Ranking on approval rate would put Night market at 1/1 = 100% and beat something four of six people wanted. So eligibility gates on coverage: every current participant has to have been shown it. That's not a heuristic — it's the difference between a group decision and one person's preference with good timing.

Coverage is measured over people who can make the chosen time, so someone who isn't coming can't hold an option hostage. The organiser can lower the threshold to break a stalemate, and the UI says so when they do.

The nudge falls out for free. pending already names exactly who's blocking each option, so the reminder targets them and nobody else — and it can say what they're blocking. Someone who's answered everything is never chased.

3 · Building the night

Once the time is settled, the itinerary is a small constrained optimisation: pick up to three options, one per category, maximising total approvals subject to a budget ceiling.

The ceiling is the minimum budget among attendees, not the average. An average silently prices out whoever's broke this fortnight — precisely the failure the budget field exists to prevent, and precisely what a student won't say out loud in a group chat.

This was greedy first, and greedy is provably wrong here. On a $40 ceiling it took the $28 dinner four people voted for, then found nothing fit in the remaining $12 — while pool ($15) and trivia ($25) came to exactly $40 and carried six votes between them. One candidate per category caps the search at a few dozen combinations, so it's now exhaustive and costs nothing.

Ties break on approvals, then stop count, then price, then id. Without that last chain two equally-wanted options were separated by insertion order, so which one won depended on which was added first.

dessert is a category of its own. One stop per category is what stops a night being three dinners, but dinner-then-Yochi is a real shape a night takes, and collapsing the two made it unbookable.

4 · Where to meet

Fifty Sydney suburb centroids, a spherical centroid over the group, and candidate hubs ranked by:

cost = totalTravelMinutes + 2 × (worstTrip − meanTrip)

The fairness term is doing real work. Ranking on total alone lets a hub win by being somebody's doorstep, handing the longest trip to whoever lives furthest out — who is exactly the person who then bails. Travel time is a continuous piecewise curve: walking under 2km, buses and light rail to 8km, train beyond. An earlier version restarted each band from its own intercept, which made 2.1km score faster than 2.0km and quietly corrupted the ranking for anyone near a boundary.

Known gap. This measures geography, not rail topology, so a spread-out group scores best around Burwood or Strathfield — genuinely where the middle is, even though such a group usually ends up in the city. Half the gap closes downstream: a clubbing plan finds no venues in Burwood and drifts to Darlinghurst on its own. The other half needs the Directions API.

5 · Pasting an event in

URL parsing is pure and network-free — platform, stable id, canonical URL with tracking parameters stripped so re-pastes dedupe. Metadata fetching is gated on the parse succeeding, and that ordering is the security property, not a tidiness one.

Why that ordering matters. A server that fetches whatever URL a user hands it is a server-side request forgery hole. Paste http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/ and a naive implementation retrieves cloud credentials and hands them back. Only allow-listed public event hosts ever get a request — loopback, private ranges, IP literals, lookalike hosts like lu.ma.evil.com, and non-http schemes are all rejected structurally rather than by a blocklist.

A pasted link lands in the directory as pending_review, never published — anyone with a link could otherwise write to Ripple's curated surface. An unreadable price falls back to the group's tightest budget rather than to zero, because a ticketed event that looks free quietly blows the ceiling.

Crawling those hosts for listings is a separate job and deliberately not done here. The shape it would take: the same parser as the entry point, a per-host politeness budget, conditional requests on ETag, and the same pending_review gate — the ingestion contract is already defined, only the discovery half is missing.

6 · Delivery

Outbound messages go to an outbox queue that senders drain, rather than being pushed. That's not a preference: the WhatsApp path runs whatsapp-web.js on a machine behind a home router with no stable inbound address, so a queue is the only thing that works for both it and ordinary SMS/email senders.

Nudges are per-person, per-reason and rate-limited. Someone who never started gets "still need your times"; someone who answered but has unseen options gets "one more thing on the plan: Night market". A second nudge inside twelve hours is skipped and reported as skipped, so a plan can't be turned into a way to spam somebody.

7 · Data model

TableWhy it's shaped that way
plan_optionsOne row per option, carrying the full activity JSON so an option survives a catalogue edit unchanged.
option_answersOne row per (option, person). A row existing means seen; the column means wanted. A blob per person cannot express that difference, and that difference is the entire mechanism.
member_tokensThe link is the credential. No passwords, no accounts to answer.
invitesChannel, contact, response and nudge count — what the reminder logic reads.
directory_submissionsUnique on (platform, source_id), held for review.
outboxQueue that senders drain and acknowledge.

Runs on Cloudflare Workers and D1. Everything above is deterministic — no model is consulted to pick a time, rank an option, or compute a cost. An LLM writes one optional sentence of prose on the event brief and touches nothing else. If the key is missing the plan is identical, just less charming.

Three calls for you

Gate install before onboarding?

You asked whether requiring the app up front is worth the friction.

Recommendation: no. The whole reason the link travels is that answering costs nothing. Gating install at the top turns a thirty-second favour into an App Store trip and kills the loop that gets Ripple in front of people who don't have it. Put install at the end, where it buys something concrete — calendar sync, the reminder, and updates if the plan moves. That's the screen the prototype ends on.

How strict should coverage be?

Right now an option needs everyone who's coming to have seen it. Strictest and fairest, but one quiet person can stall a good suggestion.

Recommendation: keep it strict, add a deadline. Drop the threshold automatically once a plan is close to its date, and say so in the UI — "picked without Kez, who didn't answer in time" is honest; silently lowering the bar isn't.

Not built

  • Nothing is deployed. Needs a Cloudflare account and secrets.
  • The widget isn't compiled — no macOS toolchain here. It's source to drop into Xcode, and the first build is a real step.
  • Live venue search is off without a Places key. Without one it returns clearly-labelled samples with no invented ratings or prices.
  • Directory crawling — only user-pasted links come in.
  • Interactive widget buttons (iOS 17) would let someone clear a pending question from the home screen. Obvious next step for the delta ask.

Deterministic engine, 155 tests, no dependencies. The scheduling and ballot logic is pure and testable without a database, because its output decides what a group of people does on a Friday and it should be arguable.
