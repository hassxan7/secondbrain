> [!tip] Civly Relevance — **HIGH**
> This is the clearest practitioner account we have of where the manual hours actually go: option-generation and space-planning re-checking, deterministic code rules held in the designer's head, and scan-to-model conversion that Polycam sells for ~US$100 and probably fulfils with offshore human drafters. Three directly implementable things fall out: a **clearance/space-planning checker** that re-tests a layout on every change, a **free scan → floor plan → model** path into our existing image processor, and confirmation that **option variation (2–3 demolition/layout schemes per project) is the repetitive unit of work**, not the drawing itself. It also corrects an assumption: he does **not** want cladding replacement automated, and he deliberately hides polish from clients at sketch stage for pricing reasons.

## Notes

### Who and what he does
- Practising Sydney architect. Portfolio includes Mirvac work and the New Sydney Fish Market; currently mostly residential renovations plus apartment work with developers.
- Works almost entirely in **Revit**, in **3D**, even for small renovations. Exception: highly technical junction details, which are drafted as 2D drafting views in Revit.
- Renders/visualisation via **Enscape** (sometimes Twinmotion); increasingly uses image models for presentation imagery. Was told: check **Design Arena** for which image model is currently best; **Nano Banana Pro** recommended at time of call.
- Proposals and design-brief documents are laid out **manually in InDesign** — "super old school, but the tool is very reliable". Acknowledged as automatable.

### His end-to-end process
1. **Client meeting → design brief.** Formulaic question set (budget, timeframe, who uses the space). Takes notes, then uses AI to help write the brief document. The brief states the problem and how he'll solve it, and **becomes part of the contract**.
2. **Options.** Produces ~3 options, commonly distinguished by **extent of demolition** (option 1 demolish a lot → option 3 demolish only the kitchen). Each option is a **separate full 3D Revit model**. Even the cheapest option is expected to deliver most of what the client asked for.
3. **Presentation.** 2D-looking sheets generated from the 3D model, plus renders. Deliberately *not* shown as a 3D model at sketch stage.
4. **DD / documentation → details → construction.** Details get marked up with the site engineer.

### The manual-thought bottleneck (the core pain)
- For each option he must re-check the whole layout by eye: kitchen-to-bench distance, space between couches, clearance around dining chairs, whether a bathtub fits, whether a new stair fits, what happens if a column must be retained, what if there's an office / no office.
- His words: *"I can tell if it works by looking at it, but you could just measure the distances... you just want to test every time you change it."* → **explicit ask for a deterministic clearance checker that re-runs on every change.**
- Layout exploration is literally "shuffling things around until it works" while staying compliant — kitchen could be here, or here, or here.

### Deterministic code rules live in the designer's head
- The mistakes made are **not** clashes — they're **building regulations**. Example given: an apartment entry door must be **more than 6m from a fire stair**.
- NCC is ~900 pages across 3 volumes; nobody holds it all. (Civly has already extracted the deterministic values into a rule engine.)
- Counter-argument he raised against a purely agentic approach: a human designer is also **compressing client desires against past experience** — e.g. his boss of 20 years knows that a client who says "I know exactly what I want" doesn't. That part is not deterministic.

### Developer/apartment typology = categorised, repeatable specs
- Developers build 1/2/3-bedroom apartments (rarely studios, sometimes 4-bed).
- Every aspect is categorised by type, like car trim levels: **3-bed → double sink, always a bathtub; 2-bed → 1.5 sink, bathtub if it fits; 1-bed → single sink.**
- He is never laying out one apartment — he's laying out a **floor plate of many apartments**, all of which must conform. Applying one resolved room across many slightly different layouts is **currently 100% manual**. "There's no way to do it in Revit. It's not automated."
- Counterweight: **every site is unique** (area, boundary angles, elevation, soil, orientation), so copy-paste across projects doesn't work — "every building is bespoke, in the truest sense".

### Pre-concept / envelope work
- Before concept there is **pre-concept**: establishing the buildable envelope on a site being considered for acquisition, to prove a yield that stacks up against the feasibility. Whole roles exist just to do this, in Revit.
- Flagged as an obvious automation target ("I wouldn't want to be those guys") — subjective input is small, and Michael described the same pain independently.

### Polycam and scan-to-BIM — the direct product opening
- He scans existing spaces (e.g. a bathroom to be split into two) with **Polycam** and works from it as reference. Wobbly geometry doesn't matter much — *"it's just a reference."*
- **Polycam offers a Revit model from the scan, but you pay for it (~US$100).** His belief: *"I reckon they get draftspeople in India to do it. It's not AI."* Turnaround is overnight-ish.
- Asked whether ingesting the scan and auto-building the Revit model would help → **yes**.
- Yash's note: better accuracy is achievable with a cheap phone attachment / sensor approach — a phone scan alone is maybe **70%** of survey grade; **~95%** is reachable with a cheap attachment.
- Key limitation he raised: the scan only covers **inside the unit**, so he still can't see what's behind a wall or below a floor — e.g. whether a new bathroom's drainage lands over a corridor or over the neighbouring apartment. A full 3D survey would solve it but isn't economical on a small job.

### Site constraints and renovation complexity
- Renovation/existing-building work is where the hard constraints live: half-built abandoned structure purchased by a new developer, internals redesigned to add more apartments, **existing structure immovable**, steel-framed (unusual for apartments), fire separation between units and all service junctions extremely complicated, and the **NCC changed in the interim** so it had to meet the new code.
- Cladding: large amounts of Sydney is covered in flammable panels being stripped and replaced. Explicitly said he **would not want this automated** — "flam comes off, non-flam goes on", it's simple. (Corrects an earlier assumption from Boris.)

### Offshore drafting — why it fails, and why that's our opening
- Firms already outsource drafting overseas (India named) because it's much cheaper.
- Why it doesn't work well: **unfamiliarity with the local building code**, hard to manage across time zones, and architecture works better when people are in the same room for fast back-and-forth.
- **Print culture:** reading comprehension is materially higher on paper; drafting-error detection is *"times like 100"* worse on screen. Firms without printers (common offshore) miss gross errors.
- QA reality: the errors are not sophisticated clashes — *"the title of the drawing is not on the sheet"*, *"half the drawing is missing"*. His challenge: ask 10 firms about their QA and every one will say too many drafting errors.
- **Implication for Civly:** the bar for an automated drafter isn't world-class design judgement — it's beating an offshore drafter who doesn't know the NCC and doesn't print.

### The grad-architect analogy (how the work is actually split)
- Principal holds a compressed, near-complete structural/spatial model **in his head** (chess-master pattern-recognition analogy — sees packaged patterns, not individual pieces, so can do it fast).
- Hands it to a grad, who models it up in Revit and hands it back.
- Seeing it modelled reveals the gaps between what was in his head and what's real → refinements → back again. Better designers miss less.
- Drafting a stage of a substantial project ≈ a week to a month.
- Design time varies enormously by firm: a boutique/design-led practice may spend **6 months** on a concept, modelling and comparing 10 options a thousand times over in 3D + Enscape; a volume practice may do a house DA in **2–3 weeks** from an owner's sketch.

### Tools discussion
- **Grasshopper** is the computational-design tool in practice; **Dynamo** is effectively dead — *"I haven't heard of people using Dynamo in eight years."* Increasingly people just write code / use LLMs.
- **Rhino.Inside.Revit** runs the whole of Rhino as a plug-in inside Revit, same workspace — flagged as a possible expansion surface for Civly, since Rhino is stronger for concept and algorithmic work.

### Why architects hide polish from clients (pricing psychology)
- Architects avoid showing a 3D model at sketch stage because clients assume everything is resolved when it isn't, and get over-excited.
- Some architects model in Revit, then **make the output look hand-drawn** (historically by tracing over a print; now potentially with AI) — to preserve the perceived jump in value between stages and justify charging more for the next stage.
- Directly relevant anecdote from our own demos: an architect told us *"okay, but we still have to tell our clients it's taking this much time... we don't make enough money."* → **Speed alone can threaten the billing model; the pitch has to address value capture, not just time saved.**

### MEP / routing discussion (our pitch, his reaction)
- Our framing: MEP consultants largely do lookups plus some intuition; with enough MEP files a model can learn to route. Training method = take a finished model, delete the MEP, regenerate it, diff against the real one, iterate.
- Routing framed as a graph problem — multi-target A* ("Google Maps, but hitting every room, with supply and return"), currently via **NetworkX**, working reasonably well. He accepted the framing: *"now I actually understand that the laying out is a graph problem."*
- Chip-design analogy offered as precedent: EDA-style automatic routing/placement produced step-change improvements; the same is ~a year away for architecture. He agreed, and said he started his business partly because he could see this coming.

### Asks / next steps from the call
- He is willing to be a **pilot / guinea pig** and to have workflows built for him.
- Open offer to build a workflow for any laborious part of his process.
- Ran short on time (~15 min left) — he'd walk through the rest of his process another time.
