# Application for PASS Leader

## Opening

The first maths class I ever taught, none of the students could read the question. I had prepared a worksheet on two-digit addition and handed it out, and eleven children sat there holding paper they could not use. That was the day I learned the only rule I now teach by: **start where they are, not where you wish they were.**

I want to be a PASS Leader because that rule is the whole job. PASS is not a lecture repeated more slowly. It is a room where students work out, together, where they actually are, and then move from there. I have done that work before with the hardest possible group, and I would like to do it here.

## Empathy with students and the struggles of transition

**Situation.** I volunteered for [X months] teaching maths to Grade 3 and Grade 4 students at [orphanage name] in [location]. Most had never been inside a classroom before that year. They were placed in a grade by age, not by what they knew.

**Task.** I had to teach the Grade 3 and 4 curriculum to students who had missed everything that came before it, without making them feel behind.

**Action.** Before I taught anything I spent the first two sessions not teaching. I sat with each student and asked them to talk me through one question out loud. I was not marking them. I was listening for the exact point where the confidence dropped. For most of them it was not addition at all. It was that they did not trust that numbers meant the same thing every time.

**Result.** I stopped teaching the worksheet and started teaching counting with bottle caps, which they could hold. Within [X weeks], [X of 11] students were doing two-digit addition without help.

**Reflection.** The gap was never ability. It was that nobody had asked them where they were. First-year university students arrive in exactly the same shape. They are not behind on the content of week four, they are behind on an assumption made in week one that nobody checked. Start where they are, not where you wish they were.

## Identifying areas of difficulty and strategies to overcome them

**Situation.** Halfway through, the same three students kept failing subtraction with borrowing while the rest of the group moved on.

**Task.** I had to find the actual cause rather than repeat the method louder, and do it without holding back the other eight.

**Action.** I gave the whole group a task they could run themselves, then sat with the three. I asked them to teach the step back to me. All three could recite the rule. None could say why the ten moved. The problem was that borrowing had been taught as a trick, and a trick with no reason behind it does not survive a new question. I rebuilt it with ten bundled sticks so they could physically break a ten into ones.

**Result.** All three passed the end-of-term assessment. More useful to me, one of them started correcting another student's work, using the sticks, without being asked.

**Reflection.** Getting the right answer and understanding the method are different things, and only one of them holds up under pressure. In a PASS session I would use the same diagnostic move. Ask the student to explain the step back to me, and the real gap shows itself in about thirty seconds.

## Time management, communication, listening and interpersonal skills

I ran these sessions while studying full time and while building a business. Before that I founded and ran an agricultural trading company, which I exited in [year]. I currently co-found Civly, a construction technology startup, alongside my Commerce degree at the University of Sydney.

That combination is only possible with hard scheduling. I block fixed hours, I protect them, and I prepare before the room rather than improvising in it. For the orphanage sessions I planned each class the night before and wrote a one-line goal for it, so that if the session went sideways I still knew what the students had to walk out with.

Communication with those students taught me more than any presentation ever has. I could not rely on shared vocabulary, so I had to check understanding constantly instead of assuming it. I ask short questions and then stay quiet long enough for the answer to arrive, which is a skill I learned by getting it wrong for the first month.

## Reflecting on performance and implementing feedback

**Situation.** In my early sessions I did most of the talking. I thought a quiet room meant a room that was following.

**Task.** [Coordinator or teacher name] observed a class and told me the students were watching me solve problems rather than solving them.

**Action.** I changed the structure. I cut my own talking to under ten minutes per session and gave the rest to the students working in pairs, with me moving between them. I also stopped answering questions directly. When a student asked me something, I asked the group first.

**Result.** Participation rose sharply. By the final weeks students were bringing questions to each other before they brought them to me, which is exactly what I wanted and had been preventing.

**Reflection.** I had confused being useful with being visible. That correction is the single most relevant thing I bring to PASS.

## Understanding of PASS and facilitating without dominating

PASS works because a student who learned something six months ago remembers what it felt like not to know it. A lecturer often does not. My role as a Leader is not to be the smartest person in the room. It is to design the hour so that the room does the work, then get out of the way.

Practically, that means preparing collaborative problems rather than worked solutions, redirecting questions back to the group, watching who has not spoken and creating a low-risk way for them to, and resisting the urge to fill silence. The bottle caps worked because the students held them. The same principle applies here. Whoever is holding the problem is the one learning.

## Why I want this

I like helping people, and teaching is the most direct version of that I have found. I have seen what happens when someone is met at the level they are actually at rather than the level the syllabus assumes. It changes what they think they are capable of. That is the work I want to keep doing, and PASS is where I can do it for students in my own faculty.

Start where they are, not where you wish they were. I would be glad to bring that into a PASS room.
