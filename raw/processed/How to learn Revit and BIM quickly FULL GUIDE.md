- **Presenter's credentials:** went from Monash University graduate → BIM coordinator in 6 months; shortly after → project manager trajectory; all attributed to learning Revit correctly early
- **Why Revit specifically:** Revit is the entry point that unlocks every subsequent role: modeller → coordinator → BIM manager → project manager → senior manager / own firm

### Market State — Why Revit Is Now Mandatory (6:06–13:30)

- **Monopoly status:** Revit + Autodesk have effective monopoly in AEC; ArchiCAD firms shifting to Revit, AutoCAD firms shifting to Revit, even some Tekla (structural) firms migrating
- **Electrical contractors:** used to get away with AutoCAD; now required to know Revit to win projects (mandated by clients and head contractors)

  - 2015–2020: gradual adoption as collaboration benefits became clear
  - 2020 (COVID): construction design moved entirely remote; BIM became the only option → industry adoption accelerated to near-universal
  - 2024: AI being integrated into Revit/ACC (automated clash detection, document scanning, AI auto-specs); Autodesk leaning into this strongly
- **2024–2029 projection:** BIM industry projected to double in size; Civly is building in a massive tailwind market
- **Client mandates:** large clients now require BIM deliverables from architects and builders at contract signing — AEC firms without BIM capability cannot win certain project types
- **Current state:** most companies have made the shift; the few still on AutoCAD/ArchiCAD are losing projects; anyone not learning Revit is actively falling behind relative to the market

### Three Career Scenarios — Growing vs Stagnating (13:30–22:00)

**Variable 1:** how fast the industry is growing
**Variable 2:** how fast you are learning new skills

Three outcomes:
1. **You learn faster than industry grows:** competitive advantage, ahead of curve, promoted faster than peers
2. **You learn at the same rate as industry growth:** you feel like you're growing (doing more complex work), but you're actually stagnant — your relative position in the market hasn't improved; you look like you're progressing but you're just keeping pace with the baseline
3. **You don't learn new skills while the industry grows:** you feel stagnant but you're actually going backwards — there's now a gap between your skill set and what the market needs; you can't leave your current job because no one else will hire you at your current level; you feel trapped

**The invisible deterioration:** people in scenario 3 often don't realise it for 5+ years; they "feel stagnant" but don't diagnose why — the gap between them and the industry has quietly widened

**The Three Boats:**
- **Boat 1 (Revit beginner):** knows how to model a house from YouTube tutorials; rowboat — going hard but slow; applying to jobs and getting rejected
- **Boat 2 (Revit proficient + construction documents):** knows floor plans, elevations, sections, wall details, partition plans, proper graphical presentation, scale, line weights; can cruise; starting to get job interviews
- **Boat 3 (Full BIM skill set):** Boat 2 + MEP basics + structural basics + coordination skill set + multidisciplinary understanding; jet ski — motor takes you forward without having to row

**Your job:** get from Boat 1 to Boat 3 as fast as possible; Boat 1 to 2 can happen in weeks; Boat 2 to 3 is the harder jump (needs real coordination understanding)

**Why Revit mastery alone doesn't work:**
- Every manager and BIM lead you want to work with has: (a) Revit proficiency + (b) coordination skill + (c) multidisciplinary skill set — all three together
- Becoming a Revit "expert" (mastering filters, Dynamo, complex families) without the coordination/multi-discipline layer is like a manager who has perfect attention-to-detail but can't communicate — one skill out of three, not enough for promotion
- The promotion you're looking for requires your boss's skill set, not a deeper version of what you already have

### What 95% Do Wrong — Three Incorrect Approaches (35:55–47:37)

**Incorrect Approach 1: Volume without depth**
- Modelling 3–4 houses from YouTube tutorials, putting them on a portfolio, applying to jobs
- Analogy: handing a hiring manager a sheet of lyrics to a song instead of playing it; they can see the words but they can't feel whether you know music
- What actually signals competence: a single, deep construction set of documents — elevations, sections, proper sheets with scale and line weights, wall details, partition plans — because when you discuss these in an interview, the depth is immediately obvious (like hearing Beyoncé sing 5 seconds vs reading her lyrics)
- Hiring managers know within minutes of speaking to you whether you've actually worked in the industry — there's no faking this

**Incorrect Approach 2: Single-discipline learning, no multidisciplinary context**
- Learning Revit only for your own discipline (e.g. only architectural modelling); not learning the basics of MEP, structural, or construction process
- If you're put in a coordination meeting and someone discusses duct routing, pipe slopes, or steel clashes, you have no context — you can't contribute and you don't get the opportunity to
- Multidisciplinary context is what lets you link things together and speak with conviction about coordination decisions

**Incorrect Approach 3: Applying to open job listings at low volume**
- Applying to 5–10 jobs that are currently listed → competing with everyone in the market for the same jobs
- Both volume is low AND competition is high → formula for failure
- People use this approach then blame the market: "construction is slow," "the quarter is tough," "I don't know the standards yet," "I need to learn Dynamo first" — these are all false excuses; the real reason is low volume + wrong targeting

### The Correct Path — Step by Step (47:38 to end)

**Step 1: Build ONE high-quality construction set of documents**
- One office building, modelled thoroughly: floor plans, elevation views, section views, 3D views, proper sheets with scale and title blocks, wall partition plan, wall details, proper line weights and graphical quality
- Going deep on one project is infinitely more valuable than 4 shallow house models
- This alone positions you as someone who understands the construction process — the context reveals itself immediately in conversation

**Step 2: Learn MEP + structural + construction process basics**
- Not expert level — you need to know enough to understand coordination meetings, read plans across disciplines, and speak intelligently about how MEP and structure interact
- Key things to understand: how much ceiling space different MEP services need, when a riser needs to grow, how pipes penetrate slabs, how ductwork transitions, what BIM coordination meetings actually discuss
- Sources: YouTube, coordination guides, talking to people in the industry

**Step 3: Apply the volume job acquisition strategy**
- **Apply to 100% of available job listings** that match (not 10%); most people skip ~90% of available listings for various reasons (location, name brand, etc.)
- **Cold-call 30 companies per day** — even companies not currently advertising open roles
  - Say: "I'm looking for a BIM coordinator/modeller role, I have experience in [X], can I speak to your BIM manager?"
  - Most say no; that's fine — track them in a spreadsheet, follow up in 3 months
  - One out of every ~150–200 calls = interview; at 30 calls/day → ~5 per month → in 30 days you have interviews lined up
  - Companies that say no often keep your CV on file; presenter got his current job from a cold call made 3 years before he was hired
- **Key difference:** you are the only one calling non-hiring companies; competition is essentially zero; volume is entirely in your control

**The hiring psychology:**
- When you have the skill set and get into an interview, you are confident — you can answer the targeted questions (explain a clash detection scenario, describe how you'd set up ACC permissions, explain MEP routing logic)
- Confidence grounded in real skill cannot be faked; hiring managers know within minutes whether you've actually done the work
- At that point, lack of formal experience becomes irrelevant — you have the knowledge equivalent to someone with 4+ years of industry experience
